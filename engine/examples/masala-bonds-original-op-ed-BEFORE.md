# Built on Borrowed Money: Why Kerala's New Government Wants to Rein In the Borrowing That Built

*Transparency note: This is explanatory journalism written openly from the side of public goods and the people who depend on them. It argues a point of view and tells you when it is doing so. Facts are sourced and confirmed; interpretation is marked as interpretation. Confidence: facts — Confirmed; framing — the writer's reasoned view.*

---

Every serious enterprise on earth runs on borrowed money. Companies raise debt to grow; nations borrow against their own future. The stock market itself is institutionalised betting on potential — capital handed over today against what something might become tomorrow. Borrowing to build is not a flaw in the system. It is the system.

Which is what makes the argument now consuming Kerala so revealing. When a Communist state government borrowed to build, it was branded reckless. The question worth asking is why the same act reads as prudent for a corporation and sinister for a state that spent the money on hospitals and schools.

## The bell at the exchange

In 2019, Chief Minister Pinarayi Vijayan — a CPI(M) Politburo member whose politics was formed in trade-union halls, not boardrooms — rang the opening bell at the London Stock Exchange. He was marking the listing of "masala bonds," rupee debt raised by the Kerala Infrastructure Investment Fund Board (KIIFB), making Kerala the first sub-sovereign entity in India to borrow on that market.

The money funded roads, bridges, school buildings, hospitals and flood-resilience works in a state repeatedly battered by climate disaster. Why borrow rather than spend from revenue? Because an Indian state does not control its own purse: the Union government sets the borrowing ceiling and decides how much fiscal room each state gets. KIIFB was a way to keep building when that room was tight. It borrowed at a premium of roughly a percentage point over ordinary government rates — the price, in effect, of building anyway.

## The white paper

In May 2026 the Congress-led UDF returned to power, and Chief Minister and Finance Minister V.D. Satheesan tabled a 195-page white paper, "Kerala's Fiscal Health: A Status Report." It described a debt of more than five lakh crore rupees, recast KIIFB as a "parallel fiscal authority," called the masala-bond cost "unjustifiably high," and recommended a forensic audit, an end to off-budget borrowing, and disinvestment, privatisation or closure of "non-strategic" public enterprises.

*Interpretation:* Every incoming government in India commissions a document like this, painting its predecessor's books in the darkest available ink — it lowers expectations and pre-justifies whatever comes next. That doesn't make the debt figures false; it means the framing is a political choice, and "the house is on fire, only drastic measures will do" is a familiar way to make privatisation look like rescue rather than ideology.

It's worth noting the committee did not call to abolish KIIFB — it recommended preserving its capabilities under tighter budgetary control. The fight is less about whether the vehicle works than about who holds the keys.

## The hospitals, and who is buying them

Kerala's celebrated healthcare model is already shifting under the same pressure. Over five years, roughly 1,300 small clinics and more than 400 small hospitals have closed even as the number of private hospitals has grown — consolidation, not contraction. Increasingly the buyers are the largest pools of foreign capital on earth: Blackstone holds stakes in Aster DM and KIMS Health; KKR has acquired Baby Memorial in Kozhikode and Meitra. Private equity typically buys to exit in five to seven years at a higher valuation, which in practice means more revenue per bed and a tilt toward higher-margin procedures.

This tide was rising before the present government — the financialisation of Kerala's wards is a years-long story, and several of these deals predate the UDF. But that is why the white paper matters: into a sector already tilting toward corporate ownership, the new government drops a document recommending disinvestment and a retreat of the state — while insisting in public that nothing of the sort is happening. The health minister has called privatisation reports "fake news." *Interpretation:* when the public reassurance and the documented direction of travel point opposite ways, it is reasonable to weigh the direction more heavily than the press conference.

## Who actually pays

Here is the most concrete fact. When Kerala's finances tightened, what was cut was not the salary bill or the pension bill — it was development expenditure, the spending that builds clinics, schools and roads. Plan allocations for Scheduled Castes, Scheduled Tribes, Other Backward Classes and religious minorities fell as a share of the total.

*Interpretation:* austerity is never neutral; it has a direction, and here the direction ran downward, toward those with the least cushion. The affluent already have private hospitals and private schools. It is the ordinary Malayali for whom the public clinic and the public school are not a backup but the whole of their security.

## The money behind the medicine

There's an uncomfortable fact for every side here. When the electoral-bond scheme was struck down in 2024 and the data released, pharmaceutical and hospital companies — several under tax or drug-quality investigation at the time — turned out to have funded political parties, Congress among them, via firms like Torrent Pharma and Natco and the Yashoda hospital chain.

The honest caveat, which partisan versions omit: the BJP received far more of this money than anyone else, and the largest hospital donor sent its biggest share to the BRS, not Congress. This was a system-wide entanglement of politics with private-healthcare capital, not a Congress peculiarity. *Interpretation:* that breadth is the point. If every pole of the establishment is funded by the same industry, none can be fully trusted to defend the public hospital against it — which is the lens through which a privatisation-leaning white paper deserves to be read.

## The optics

The new Chief Minister also took an undisclosed chartered flight to Mangaluru before his swearing-in. The opposition alleges — and he flatly denies — that the trip involved a meeting with a representative of the Adani group; he says it was personal. The allegation is unproven and is recorded here only as an allegation with his denial attached. *Interpretation:* even setting the disputed meeting aside, the early optics invite questions about which direction the new government's gaze is turning.

## The honest objection

The critics are not inventing the debt, and they are right that the masala-bond paperwork and consultancy costs should be opened and audited. Transparency is a public good, and the Left should welcome scrutiny rather than fear it. There is a real distinction, though, between auditing a tool and confiscating it. A forensic audit is fair. Folding KIIFB's agility back under the same fiscal constraints that prompted the workaround is a different thing.

## The takeaway

When a left government borrowed to build hospitals, it was called a debt trap. When private capital borrows to *own* those hospitals, it is called a market. The difference was never the debt — it was who the building was for. Watch which projects get finished over the next five years, and watch who ends up owning the wards. The balance sheet may look cleaner. The question worth holding onto is who paid for the tidiness, and who gets to own what was built for everyone.
