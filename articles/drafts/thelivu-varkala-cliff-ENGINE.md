# Why Kerala's Most Famous Cliff Is Coming Apart — and What a Court Just Did About It

*From Thelivu — explanatory journalism on the side of ordinary people. It argues a view and tells you when it's doing so. Facts are sourced and confirmed; the opinion is mine and is flagged. Drafted with AI assistance, reviewed by a human editor.*

---

The warning signs went up before the tourists noticed anything was wrong. Along the scenic footpaths of the Varkala cliff — the red laterite wall that drops to the Arabian Sea an hour north of Thiruvananthapuram, the one on every Kerala postcard — small boards now caution visitors about the ground beneath them. The cliff is moving. Slowly, unevenly, but it is moving.

Here's why, and it's worth understanding because it's not a metaphor. The top of the cliff is a hard cap of laterite, three to four metres thick. Underneath it sits much softer stuff — sandstone and carbonaceous clay that was never built to carry the weight above it. When water finds a crack in the laterite and seeps down, it weakens those soft lower layers. Enough water, enough weight on top, a hard enough monsoon, and sections of cliff slump or slide. This is not a risk someone is inventing for a court case. It is geology, and it is already happening.

## What stands to be lost

This is not just a pretty view. The Geological Survey of India has declared Varkala cliff a National Geological Monument, and last year it entered UNESCO's tentative list of World Heritage Sites — it is the largest geo-heritage site in Kerala. But strip away the honours and it is something more useful than scenic: the cliff works as a natural aquifer and water-harvesting system for the coastal communities below it, its slopes hold a particular microhabitat, and the reefs offshore that it helps sustain matter to local fishers. And for thousands of Varkala residents, the tourism the cliff draws is simply the household income. So the thing at risk is rare on several axes at once — geological, ecological, economic. You don't get another one.

## The squeeze, from several directions at once

What's pushing the cliff toward the edge isn't one culprit, and that's the honest part of the story. A warming, more turbulent Arabian Sea is part of it. So is a grim irony of coastal engineering: large port construction further south has eroded the beaches at Kovalam and Vizhinjam, which pushed more tourists — and more tourism-building — onto Varkala.

Then there are the choices closer to home. Cliff-top eateries and resorts, many without proper waste systems, run concealed pipes that pump wastewater out over the cliff face — which is to say, they are quietly feeding the exact process, water into the cracks, that brings the cliff down. Construction has spread along the top with thin regard for what the ground can hold.

And then the government arrived to help, and made it worse. The Union government sanctioned ₹25 crore for a "Varkala Dakshin Kashi" project under its Swadesh Darshan tourism scheme, to develop the site near the cliff-top Janardanaswamy temple. According to the public-interest petition that followed, the work went ahead without proper planning, without mandatory local-body clearances, without expert guidance — earthmovers operating on a fragile cliff, digging within the temple premises that activists said was destabilising the very ground the project was meant to celebrate.

## When the executive won't, the courts do

It fell to a handful of activists and a court to apply the brakes the administration didn't. Acting on the petition, a division bench of the Kerala High Court ordered an interim stay on the Dakshin Kashi project, citing environmental violations, procedural lapses, and the unplanned spending of that ₹25 crore of public money — and earlier intervened to stop the digging near the temple. The National Green Tribunal has separately been pressed to act on the cliff's broader decline.

That's worth sitting with for a second. The body that finally moved to protect a National Geological Monument was not the department that runs it, or the local government, or the tourism ministry that funded the project. It was a court, responding to citizens, after the fact.

## The view from the cliff's edge

*Here's where I'll be plain about it.* The temptation is to make this a story about villains — greedy resorts, a careless scheme. Resist it, because the cliff doesn't care about anyone's motives. The resort owner pumping wastewater is usually trying to run a business in a town that lives on tourism; the Kashi project was, on paper, meant to honour the place. Nobody set out to wreck Varkala.

That's exactly what makes it a harder, more useful lesson than a morality tale. A rare and fragile commons was treated, by commerce and government alike, as if it were ordinary ground you could build on, dig into, and drain onto — and the cumulative result is a monument that is physically dissolving. The failure here isn't ideology. It's competence and enforcement: clearances skipped, waste rules unpoliced, weight and water unmanaged on a structure everyone knew was fragile.

And the fixes are correspondingly boring and well-known. The Geological Survey's own people have said it: proper drainage so water doesn't reach the soft layers, less weight and traffic on the cliff top, an end to unauthorised construction, and someone actually enforcing the rules already on the books. None of it is glamorous. None of it needs ₹25 crore.

The court has bought Varkala some time. But a stay is a pause, not a plan — it stops the digging, it doesn't fix the drainage. The real test isn't the next hearing date. It's whether the unglamorous work of protecting the cliff gets done before a hard monsoon arrives and makes the decision for everyone. The cliff is keeping its own schedule, and it does not adjourn.
