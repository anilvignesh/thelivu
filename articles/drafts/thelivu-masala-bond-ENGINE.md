# Kerala Built With Borrowed Money. Now Comes the Reckoning — and the Double Standard

*From Thelivu — explanatory journalism on the side of ordinary people. It argues a view and tells you when it's doing so. Facts are sourced and confirmed; the opinion is mine and is flagged as such. Drafted with AI assistance, reviewed by a human editor.*

---

Walk into a government school in Kerala that was a leaking, half-collapsed building five years ago and is now a proper place to learn. Sit in the waiting room of a taluk hospital that finally has the equipment it lacked. Drive a road that used to flood. Somebody paid for that. The previous Left government paid for it with borrowed money — and that borrowing is now on trial.

The story being sold is simple: the new Congress-led government released a white paper exposing how the reckless Communists ran up debt and gambled with "masala bonds." It's a clean morality tale. It also skips the two questions that actually matter to you: *what did the money buy, and is the outrage aimed where it belongs?*

## What it bought

Let's not be coy about this, because the white paper's defenders often are. Through the Kerala Infrastructure Investment Fund Board (KIIFB), the state financed close to 600 completed school buildings — out of roughly 970 it set out to rebuild. It bankrolled the fibre-optic network behind treating internet as a public right, the e-health records system, expanded IT parks, and a long list of roads, bridges and hospitals. KIIFB became one of the biggest financiers of public infrastructure the state has had. These are not slides in a presentation. They are buildings your neighbours' children study in.

That matters because it answers the first question honestly: this borrowing did not vanish into anyone's pocket. It went into things the public uses. Hold on to that, because the rest of the story is going to get more uncomfortable — for both sides.

## What it cost, honestly

Here is where the public deserves the truth even when it's inconvenient for the side that built the schools. The whole pitch for KIIFB was that it could borrow more cheaply than the government. The white paper's data says that simply wasn't true — in every year but the COVID-hit 2020-21, KIIFB's borrowing cost ran about 1.5 percentage points above the state's own rate, roughly ₹420 crore a year in extra interest. The 2019 masala-bond issue cost ₹11 crore that auditors call "unjustifiably high," with documents they say they were never shown. A fifth of all the money went to Kannur — the then-Chief Minister's home district. And nearly 68% of it went to roads, industry and health, while schools, water and sanitation were *not* the priority the speeches implied.

You can love the schools and still want that audited. A public-interest reader should. Borrowing at an unjustified premium, with opaque consultancy bills, skewed toward one district, is exactly the kind of thing that ought to be opened up — whoever did it. On the question of cost and stewardship, the white paper has a real case.

## Now hold it up against the country

But here is the part the morality tale leaves out, and it's the part that should make every ordinary Keralite pause. Kerala borrowed about ₹42,000 crore through KIIFB to build public assets — every rupee named, traced, and now forensically audited, with the Chief Minister's bank records and his family's transactions hauled in, and the BJP calling it a "Ponzi scheme."

Meanwhile, across the same decade, India's banks quietly wrote off about ₹16 lakh crore in bad loans — roughly thirty times KIIFB's entire liability — and more than half of that, around ₹9 lakh crore, was large industry and services. Who benefited? You're not allowed to know. The RBI does not disclose which companies' loans were written off; their identities are shielded by law. There is no forensic audit, no "Ponzi" headline, no bank records dragged into the open.

Be precise, because precision is the point: a write-off is not a waiver. Those borrowers technically still owe the money, and banks claim to pursue recovery — though for years they clawed back only a small fraction. And the real, full *forgiveness* in Indian banking mostly goes the other way, to farmers in distress, not tycoons. So this isn't a claim that named billionaires had their debts cancelled — that's a rumour, not a fact, and the data to prove it is deliberately hidden.

The honest point is sharper than the rumour anyway: **the scrutiny is upside down.** A state that borrowed to build for the public is stripped naked and called a fraud, while far larger sums of mostly-private bad debt are written off behind a confidentiality wall. The schoolchild's government gets a forensic audit. The defaulting corporate gets Section 45E.

## What's actually happening

So strip away the theatre and here's the read from where most of us stand. The masala bonds built real things and cost more than they should have — both are true, and the public is owed both truths. The white paper is a legitimate audit *and* the new government's runway toward something else: it praises KIIFB as a "bold innovation" it won't dismantle, then leans, in the same breath, toward private capital — a turn the LDF calls a privatisation set-up, and which a right-wing national outlet openly cheers as exactly that.

The case *for* what Kerala did is not naïve, and it deserves stating plainly: good infrastructure is seed capital. A literate, skilled state with connected schools, working hospitals and universal internet compounds those advantages for a generation. Betting borrowed money on that backbone can be sound the way a mortgage is sound — if the price is fair and the books are honest. KIIFB's problem was never that it built. It was the price and the opacity. Fix those, and the bet is defensible.

The danger now is not the audit. An audit is good; you deserve honest books as much as you deserve hospitals. The danger is that a fair audit becomes the excuse for the quiet project — pulling the state back from building for people who can't build for themselves, and handing the gap to private money that builds only where it pays. And the danger is that we save all our outrage for the ₹42,000 crore that built schools, and none for the ₹16 lakh crore that didn't.

Watch two things over the next five years: whether the schools still get rebuilt and the roads still get finished — and whether anyone, ever, demands of the country's vanished corporate lakhs the forensic audit Kerala just got. The balance sheet will look tidier. The question is whose tidiness, and at whose cost.
