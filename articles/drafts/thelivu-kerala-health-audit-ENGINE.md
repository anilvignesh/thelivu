# Kerala Has India's Best Health Outcomes — and Its Highest Medical Bills. The Two Are Connected.

*From Thelivu — explanatory journalism on the side of ordinary people. It argues a view and tells you when it's doing so. Facts are sourced and confirmed; the opinion is mine and is flagged. Drafted with AI assistance, reviewed by a human editor.*

---

Kerala holds two records at once, and almost no one talks about them together.

The famous one: a baby born in Kerala is more likely to reach its first birthday than a baby born in the United States. Its infant mortality rate is 5 per 1,000 against a national average of 25, its life expectancy is the country's highest, and its health-worker density leads India. This is real, it is decades in the making, and it is built on something specific — *primary* care. Village clinics, immunisation, institutional births, the ASHA worker who knows your street. That is the Kerala model, and it deserves its fame.

The record nobody brags about: Kerala also has the **highest out-of-pocket medical spending in India.** Households here pay around ₹7,889 per person a year at the point of care — about 59% of all health spending in the state comes straight from people's pockets. By one expert committee's reckoning, more than 90% of Kerala's health *spending* happens in the private sector. And the bills bite: an estimated 12% of rural and 8% of urban households have been pushed into poverty paying for healthcare.

Best survival odds in the country, and the heaviest medical bills in the country. Those two facts are not a contradiction. They are the same system seen from two ends — and a quiet audit explains how they connect.

## What the audit actually says, read correctly

In January 2025 the Comptroller and Auditor General tabled a performance audit of Kerala's public health infrastructure. The headlines, where there were any, said "doctor shortage." The real finding is more specific, and it matters.

The primary tier — the clinics that earn Kerala its survival statistics — is largely fine: those centres ran at 94 to 98% of their sanctioned doctors. The hole is one level up. In the government medical colleges, the public *tertiary* hospitals where the seriously ill are sent, doctors in position ran at just 35 to 82% of sanctioned strength. Statewide, government hospitals were short 1,160 doctors, 978 nurses and 716 paramedics. The medicine supply chain was failing too: more than 81% of ordered drugs arrived late, and some reached the wards already expired. Regulation had gaps — unlicensed blood banks, X-ray units running without atomic-energy clearance.

Now — and this is the correction to how stories like this usually get told — *the public tertiary tier was never Kerala's crown jewel.* No government claims the medical colleges are world-class. Across India, including Kerala, specialist and tertiary care is overwhelmingly private. So the audit is not the unmasking of a fraud, and anyone who frames it that way is overreaching.

But it is something quieter and more important: it is the measure of how thin the public safety net is at exactly the level where treatment gets expensive. And that thinness has a price, and a payer.

## Follow the money to the patient

Here is the connection the two records hide in plain sight. When the public medical college can't see you — because a third to two-thirds of its specialist posts are vacant, because the drug didn't arrive — you don't stop being sick. You go private. And private tertiary care in Kerala is good, plentiful, and dear. That is why a state with India's best public *primary* care also has India's highest private medical bills. The strength at the bottom of the pyramid and the squeeze at the top are the same story.

So the honest reading isn't "the Kerala model is failing." It's narrower and sharper: the model protects you superbly until you get seriously ill, and then it hands a growing share of you to a private system that — for the poor especially — can mean a hospital bill that tips the household into debt.

## The fork in the road — and the view from the public's side

This is now a live political fight, not an abstraction. During the 2026 campaign the Congress promised a ₹25-lakh health-insurance cover; the Left's Thomas Isaac warned it would push Kerala toward an insurance-driven system that deepens dependence on private hospitals. Strip out the party colours and that is the real choice: do you fix the public tertiary tier, or do you insure people to buy private care?

*Here is the view, stated plainly.* On the evidence, the bill comes down most reliably when the *public* tier is strong enough that people don't have to go private in the first place — and studies of Kerala's existing insurance schemes find they raised hospital use without meaningfully cutting out-of-pocket costs. Insurance can shield against catastrophe, and that's worth having; the Congress case for financial protection isn't empty. But insurance that routes patients into private hospitals without building public capacity tends to subsidise the private bill, not shrink it. The audit's vacancies and supply failures are an argument for *filling posts and fixing the public pharmacy* — the unglamorous public-investment fixes — before they become an argument for outsourcing the sick.

The thing to watch over the next two budgets isn't the speeches. It's one number: out-of-pocket expenditure. If the government fills the medical-college vacancies and the bill falls, the public tier is being repaired. If instead the talk is all insurance and "private participation" while OOPE stays the highest in India, then the famous model will keep saving Kerala's babies — and keep sending its families to pay, at the worst possible moment, out of their own pockets.
